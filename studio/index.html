Background

Who you're actually hiring.

I started out building database products for manufacturing companies — the software that runs a shop floor and stops it when it's wrong. Then years as a consultant in data engineering and SAP migration: moving other people's critical systems, on a deadline, without losing anything on the way across. In between I evaluated public programs for technical validity and wrote the specifications they were measured against — which is where I learned to read a claim and ask what evidence would actually have to exist for it to be true. Since 2017 I've been a solutions engineer for enterprise data platforms, translating architecture for the people who have to sign for it.

Every one of those phases is one of the three services on this page. Building products is the sprint. Migrating and finishing other people's systems is the rescue. Evaluating technical claims and writing the spec is what a founder actually needs from a fractional CTO in a diligence call. The sales engineering is the part that makes all of it legible to someone who doesn't write code.

Language models were my master's specialization before there was a market for it. I built them by hand, from the mathematics up, back when the open question was still whether a model could hold a sentence together. That means I can tell you where the current generation genuinely earns its place in a product — and where it's an expensive way to do something a lookup table does better. Increasingly that's the most valuable conversation a non-technical founder can have.

The two products above were built with that background, start to finish, by one person. They're the argument: production infrastructure, live money, published security, and six rounds of adversarial review — delivered on a schedule.

How I work

Opinions arrive before invoices.

01 — Scope

The hard question comes first

Before anything gets built I'll tell you where the plan is thin — the market that's smaller than the headline, the feature that's a company rather than a sprint. Cheaper as a conversation than as code.

02 — Build

Production, not prototype

Auth, payments, encrypted secrets and a test suite are in the first version, not the someday version. A demo that can't take money isn't a product; it's an expensive slide.

03 — Review

Everything gets attacked before it ships

Independent review passes against the code as an adversary would read it: entitlement paths, webhooks, deletion, admin routes. Findings get written down and fixed, not quietly closed.

04 — Handover

You own it, and can prove it

Your repository, your cloud account, your keys. Documented conventions, a runbook, and a deploy you can execute yourself. No hostage architecture.
